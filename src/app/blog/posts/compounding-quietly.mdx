Most career advice on the internet is written for people who enjoy rewriting their LinkedIn headline every eighteen months. There is nothing wrong with that. Markets reward mobility, negotiation, and fresh titles. What gets discussed less—because it does not fit a viral thread—is the slower bet: **staying long enough in the right place for equity and reputation to do real work**.

This note is about that bet. It is not investment advice, tax guidance, or a promise that your employer’s stock will go up. It is a frame that has helped me think about **optionality**, **taxes**, and **what “rich” even means** when your primary asset is your skill.

## Freedom is a balance sheet and a calendar

Money matters, but it is not the whole story. A modest recurring product income in a low cost-of-living city can feel freer than a large salary that owns your evenings and weekends. Many countries now offer remote-friendly visas; geography is less of a cage than it used to be. The useful question is not “What is the biggest number?” but **“What life does this income buy me, and what does it cost in stress and time?”**

If that resonates, you are already thinking in terms of **optionality** rather than vanity metrics.

## The bar is higher than “good enough”

A comfortable engineering job is, statistically, a win. If you want outcomes that look like **early retirement, deep savings, or the ability to walk away on your own timeline**, you are asking for something rarer. That usually means **unusually good execution** over a long arc—not a single heroic sprint.

Public examples of senior IC compensation often show a pattern: **base salary is only part of the picture**. Restricted stock and long vesting schedules can dominate total compensation at mature tech companies. The lesson is not “everyone should work at a giant.” It is simpler: **where equity is material, time in seat and level progression matter as much as your LeetCode speed**.

## When switching jobs helps cash—and starves the golden handcuffs

Changing employers can reset your base pay upward quickly. It can also **reset or dilute unvested grants**, push you to the back of a new vesting cliff, and fragment your narrative if you hop too fast for anyone to trust you with ambiguous scope.

There is no universal rule. There *is* a useful heuristic: **if your wealth-building thesis is equity at a company you respect, optimize for vesting and impact, not just the next signing bonus**. If your thesis is maximizing liquid cash in the short term, the opposite may be true. Know which game you are playing.

## If you choose the “stay and build” path, build skills that compound

The following habits are boring on purpose. They are the kind of thing you can do while employed that **raise the ceiling on promotions, scope, and trust**—which is how equity-heavy paths actually pay off.

### Read in depth, not only in feeds

Articles and short videos are fine for orientation. **Books and longer-form sources** still train judgment in a way algorithmic timelines do not. Depth shows up in how you explain tradeoffs, estimate risk, and mentor others.

### Write like your career depends on it—because it does

Design docs, RFCs, postmortems, and crisp email are **force multipliers**. Writing clarifies thinking. It also makes your impact legible to people who do not sit next to you in stand-up.

### Automate the small things

Keyboard fluency, scripting repetitive work, and tight feedback loops are not vanity. They are **respect for your future self** and for teammates who read your code.

### Take end-to-end quality seriously

Teams notice engineers who care whether the product actually works for a user—not only whether the unit tests are green. **Reliable automation at the boundaries of the system** (where users live) is still undervalued in many organizations. That is an opportunity if you are willing to invest.

## Keep your eyes open

Loyalty without discernment is risky. **A promising startup can mint life-changing equity; it can also evaporate.** A stable employer can fund your life while you build something on the side—or it can slowly erode your curiosity. Revisit your thesis every year: **Is this place still earning my best years?**

If the answer is yes, compounding quietly is a legitimate strategy. If the answer is no, take pride in leaving with data, savings, and relationships intact—and then choose the next game deliberately.
